How to Write Title Tags That Get Clicked

4 Aug 2026·7 min read

The title tag is the headline of your search result. It's what convinces someone scanning Google to click on your page instead of the one above or below it. Getting it right matters for rankings and for the traffic those rankings generate.

What a title tag is

The title tag is an HTML element in the <head> of a page:

<title>How to Write Title Tags That Get Clicked (2026 Guide) — Bisyri</title>

It serves three purposes simultaneously:

  1. Search result headline: the blue clickable text shown in Google search results (though Google may rewrite it)
  2. Browser tab label: the text shown in the browser tab when the page is open
  3. SEO signal: one of Google's strongest on-page signals for understanding what a page is about

It's distinct from the H1 — the on-page visible heading. They should be closely aligned but don't have to be identical. The title tag is the search result headline; the H1 is the article or page heading that visitors see after they click through.

The right length

Google displays titles based on pixel width, not character count — but as a practical guide, aim for 50–60 characters. Titles longer than this are typically truncated with an ellipsis (…) in search results, which cuts off the end of the title and can obscure important information.

This is a constraint, not a target. If your title naturally falls at 45 characters and fully describes the page, don't pad it. If the most accurate title is 63 characters, it's usually fine to trim to 58 — but don't shorten it so much that it becomes vague or misleading.

Use the free meta title generator to check the character count and pixel width of any title before publishing.

One common mistake: packing so much into the title to hit 60 characters that it becomes unreadable. "Web Design SEO Mistakes 2026 Small Business Guide UK" is 60 characters but reads poorly. "7 Web Design Mistakes That Tank Your SEO Rankings" is 51 characters and reads like a headline a human would want to click.

Keyword placement

Include your primary target keyword in the title tag — ideally towards the beginning. Google weights words earlier in the title slightly more, and from a user perspective, matching the search term early confirms to the searcher that the result is relevant before they've read the rest of the title.

This doesn't mean the keyword has to be the very first word. It means: don't bury it. "Guide to Writing Title Tags for SEO in 2026" is fine. "The Complete Comprehensive Beginner's Guide to Everything You Need to Know About Writing SEO Title Tags" is not — the keyword is late and the title is stuffed.

Use the keyword once, naturally. Repeating it ("Title Tags: Title Tag SEO Guide to Writing Title Tags") triggers a rewrite from Google and reads as spam to human searchers.

Writing for clicks, not just rankings

Ranking is a means to an end. A title that ranks position 3 but has a 15% CTR beats a title that ranks position 2 with a 6% CTR. CTR is influenced by how compelling the title looks to the searcher — not just whether it contains the right keyword.

Techniques that improve CTR without being clickbait:

  • Be specific. "How to Speed Up Your Website" is weaker than "7 Changes That Cut Page Load Time in Half". Specific numbers, specific outcomes, specific methods all signal credibility and make the result look more useful.
  • Match search intent exactly. Someone searching "how to write a title tag" wants instructions. "What Is a Title Tag" is a worse match than "How to Write a Title Tag" even if both pages cover the same information. The phrasing matters.
  • Use the year where appropriate. "SEO Guide (2026)" or "2026 Guide" signals freshness. For time-sensitive topics (algorithm changes, best practices, tool features), the year tells the searcher the content is current. Don't add a year to evergreen content where it adds nothing.
  • Front-load the value. What's in it for the reader? "How to Fix Slow Page Speed — PageSpeed Insights Guide" puts the benefit first and the method second. A searcher sees the payoff before they read the how.
  • Use parentheses or brackets for context. "(Step-by-Step)", "[Free Template]", "(Without Paid Tools)" — these add useful information in a visually distinct way that stands out in the search result.
The test: read your title out loud and ask whether a real person — not a search engine — would find it compelling. If it sounds robotic or stuffed, rewrite it.

Where to put your brand name

For most pages: brand name at the end, separated by a dash or pipe character.

The brand name takes up characters and often adds little value for a user who doesn't know you yet. Putting it at the end maximises the space at the start of the title for the keyword and the actual page description.

The exception: your homepage and any explicitly brand-focused pages. If someone searches your business name, they should see your brand prominently.

Why Google rewrites your titles — and how to stop it

Google rewrites approximately 20% of title tags with a title it considers more accurate or useful. This happens when:

  • The title doesn't accurately reflect the page content
  • The title is too long and gets cut off
  • The title is keyword-stuffed or spammy
  • The title is too generic or vague ("Home", "Page 1", "Untitled Document")
  • The title matches the H1 perfectly but Google finds a better match from anchor text of inbound links

To minimise rewrites:

  • Write titles that accurately describe what's actually on the page
  • Keep them under 60 characters
  • Use the primary keyword once, naturally
  • Make every title unique — duplicates get rewritten
  • Align the title with the H1 (they don't need to match exactly, but they should describe the same page)

If Google keeps rewriting a specific page's title and you don't like what it's choosing: check that the page's content matches your title, and review whether Google might be pulling its rewrite from an H1 or anchor text that's more prominent. Update accordingly — a rewrite isn't necessarily wrong, but it's usually telling you something about a mismatch.

Auditing your existing title tags

Check your current title tags in Screaming Frog (free up to 500 URLs) or Search Console. Look for:

  • Missing titles: any page with no title tag. Fix immediately — these are the most damaging.
  • Duplicate titles: two pages with identical titles. Each page needs a unique title. Filter in Screaming Frog → Page Titles → Duplicates.
  • Titles over 60 characters: likely being truncated. Trim without removing key information.
  • Titles under 30 characters: probably too vague. Can usually be made more specific and compelling.
  • Generic titles: "Services", "Blog", "Contact Us" — too short and too vague. Add the primary keyword and brand name.

In Search Console, check for pages with good impressions but poor CTR (search Performance → Pages, sort by impressions, add CTR column). These are pages where your title is failing to convert visibility into clicks — often the highest-leverage pages to rewrite. Compare your position to the expected CTR for that position; anything significantly below average for your position is a candidate for a title rewrite.
