How to Get More Google Reviews (Without Violating the Guidelines)

4 Aug 2026·8 min read

Most businesses know Google reviews matter. Fewer know exactly how to get them consistently, or how to handle the ones that go badly. Here's a practical system for generating a steady flow of genuine reviews — and keeping your rating as high as it should be.

Why reviews matter for local SEO

Google uses reviews as a ranking factor in local search — the map results that appear when someone searches "web designer near me" or "plumber in Manchester". Three specific things matter:

  • Volume: more reviews signal that you're a legitimate, active business with real customers. A business with 2 reviews and a business with 200 reviews are treated differently.
  • Recency: a recent stream of reviews signals an active business. Old reviews from years ago, even if positive, carry less weight than fresh ones. This is why a one-time push for reviews isn't enough — you need a consistent flow.
  • Rating: your average star rating affects both ranking and click-through. In the local pack, users can see ratings before clicking. A 4.9 at 80 reviews beats a 4.2 at 120 reviews for most queries.

Beyond SEO, reviews act as social proof — they're often the deciding factor for someone choosing between two businesses they found online. The business with more credible, recent reviews wins the enquiry even when everything else is equal.

Get your review link

The biggest barrier to getting reviews is friction. If a customer has to search for your business, find your GBP listing, and navigate to the reviews tab, most won't bother — not because they don't want to help, but because it takes effort.

Google provides a direct review link that takes customers straight to the review box. To get it:

  1. Log in to your Google Business Profile
  2. Click your business name to open your profile
  3. Click "Ask for reviews" (in the sidebar or cards section)
  4. Google generates a short link — copy it

Alternatively, you can find it by searching for your business on Google and clicking "Get more reviews" on the business panel.

Shorten this link with Bitly or a similar service so it's easy to share verbally or in print. Save it somewhere accessible so anyone in the business can use it without hunting for it each time.

How to ask correctly

The single biggest driver of reviews is simply asking. Most satisfied customers don't leave reviews unprompted — not because they don't want to, but because it doesn't occur to them. A direct ask changes that.

What to say: be specific and make it easy. "Would you mind leaving us a quick Google review? It really helps small businesses like ours." is more effective than "Please leave a review if you have time." The specificity (Google, not just "a review") reduces the decision the customer has to make.

What not to say: don't ask for a "positive" review or "five stars" — this violates Google's guidelines and is transparently self-serving. Ask for an honest review. If your service was good, an honest review will be positive.

Make it concrete: when asking in person, show the customer the QR code or short link on the spot. When asking via message, include the direct link so they can click straight through.

Channels to use

In person — the highest conversion rate

Asking face-to-face immediately after a completed job or positive interaction is the most effective method. The customer is at peak satisfaction, the experience is fresh, and you can answer any questions about how to leave a review. If you have a tablet or phone, show the QR code and let them scan it while you're still with them.

For service businesses (tradespeople, consultants, therapists), this moment is at the end of the appointment or project handover. Train every team member to do this as part of the standard close of every job.

SMS / text message

A personalised text sent within 24 hours of a completed service has high open and response rates. Keep it short: "Hi [Name], it was great working with you on [project]. If you have 2 minutes, a Google review would mean a lot to us — [link]."

Don't use bulk SMS software to blast generic messages. A personalised message from the person who did the work performs significantly better — and feels more authentic.

Email

For businesses where email is the primary communication channel, a follow-up email 24–48 hours after completion works well. Keep the subject line simple ("Quick favour?") and the body short. One clear call to action: the review link. Don't bury it after three paragraphs of thank-you copy.

QR code on printed materials

Print your review QR code on receipts, business cards, delivery packaging, or a small card left after a service. This captures customers who thought about leaving a review but forgot. "Loved your experience? Scan to leave us a review" on a well-placed card can generate reviews days or weeks after a job.

Your website

Add a "Leave a review" link in your footer, on your contact page, or on a dedicated testimonials page. Customers who come back to your site after a positive experience are already primed — make it easy for them.

Timing your ask

Ask when satisfaction is highest — which is usually immediately after a positive experience, not weeks later. The longer you wait, the more diluted the memory becomes and the less likely the customer is to invest the effort.

For project-based work (web design, renovations, events), the moment of delivery or the sign-off call is ideal. For ongoing services, ask after a particularly good interaction or when a customer gives you verbal praise — don't wait for the contract end.

For e-commerce, the window is typically 3–7 days after confirmed delivery, when the customer has had a chance to use the product but the experience is still fresh.

Don't ask during or immediately after a problem — even if you've resolved it. Wait until the resolution is confirmed and the customer is satisfied before requesting a review.

Responding to reviews

Responding to reviews — positive and negative — is both good practice and a ranking signal. Businesses that actively engage with their reviews rank better in local search than businesses that don't respond.

Responding to positive reviews

Thank the customer by name, acknowledge something specific they mentioned (not just "thanks for the 5 stars"), and reinforce your business identity. Keep it brief — 2–3 sentences. Responses that feel genuine and personalised build trust with people reading reviews before contacting you.

Don't use a template for every response. Generic copy-paste responses ("Thank you for your kind words! We hope to see you again soon!") are obviously automated and undermine the authenticity of the reviews themselves.

Responding to negative reviews

Respond to every negative review, promptly. The goal of your response is not to win the argument — it's to show potential customers reading the review that you handle problems professionally.

The right approach:

  1. Acknowledge the customer's experience without admitting liability in a way that escalates the dispute
  2. Apologise for their disappointment — even if you believe the complaint is unfair
  3. Offer to resolve the issue offline: "Please contact us at [email] and we'll do everything we can to make this right"
  4. Keep it short — don't write a paragraph-by-paragraph rebuttal

What not to do: argue with the customer publicly, accuse them of lying, or dismiss their concern. Even if the review is factually wrong, a defensive or aggressive public response damages your credibility far more than the original review.

Handling negative reviews in more detail

If the review is fake

If you're certain a review was left by someone who was never a customer (a competitor, a disgruntled non-customer), you can flag it for removal in Google Business Profile. Click the three dots next to the review and select "Report review". Google's process is slow and the threshold for removal is high — document everything and be patient.

If the review is real but unfair

Respond professionally, state your side of the story briefly, and offer to resolve offline. You won't get the review removed, but future customers reading both the review and your response will form their own judgement. A calm, professional response often neutralises the impact of an unfair review.

The best long-term defence

A business with 150 reviews at 4.7 stars is barely affected by one 2-star review. A business with 10 reviews at 4.8 stars has its average wrecked by one bad review. Volume is your best defence — the more genuine positive reviews you have, the less any single negative one can do.

What not to do

  • Don't pay for reviews: fake reviews violate Google's guidelines and can result in your GBP listing being suspended. Google's detection has improved significantly.
  • Don't offer incentives: discounts, free products, or other benefits in exchange for reviews violate both Google's guidelines and, in some jurisdictions, consumer protection law.
  • Don't cherry-pick: asking only customers you know are happy skews your review profile. Google may detect this pattern. Ask everyone — a business confident in its service quality asks all its customers, not just the ones it's pre-screened.
  • Don't ask employees to review: fake positive reviews from staff are a guideline violation and are often detectable by the patterns in the accounts.
  • Don't use review gating: asking customers "how was your experience?" and only sending the review link to those who respond positively. This is explicitly against Google's guidelines.

Using reviews on your website

Your Google reviews are a marketing asset beyond just GBP. Display them on your website — on the homepage, the services page, or a dedicated testimonials page — as social proof for visitors who find you through search or referral.

You can embed a Google reviews widget (various third-party plugins and services offer this), or manually copy your best reviews into your site's testimonials section with the customer's name and star rating.

For structured data, you can add Review or AggregateRating schema to pages that display reviews — this can enable star ratings to appear in the regular (non-local) search results as rich results. Use the schema generator to build the markup.
